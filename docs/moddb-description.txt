Fornax is Latin for furnace, and the name of the Roman goddess of the oven
— a deity most likely invented to explain a festival that already existed. At the
Fornacalia, on the seventeenth of February, spelt was parched for sacrificial cakes, and Romans
prayed to Fornax to keep the grain from scorching. The word was borrowed once more in the 1750s,
when Lacaille hung a chemical furnace among the southern stars.

This is the third borrowing, and the most literal. Between the pit kiln and the beehive kiln
there is a long gap: the pit kiln fires four things at a time and eats firewood doing it, while
the beehive kiln wants refractory brick and an iron door, which means a bloomery first. Fornax
is the kiln that goes in between — raw mud brick, a clay grate floor, firewood or peat,
and no iron at all.

Building it
A 5x5 mud brick drum around a 3x3x4 chamber, corbelled in to a chimney neck and a draft
vent. Cob works anywhere mud brick does. Nine grate tiles form the floor the wares sit on;
they are clay-formed and then fired in a pit kiln, so you use the older technology once to
build the newer one.

Place the firebox where you want the front to be — it turns to face you — then
Ctrl + right-click it with an empty hand. A blueprint of ghost blocks shows
everything still to place, drawn with each block's own texture so you can see what goes where,
and red for anything in the way.

Firing
Load green pottery or raw bricks onto the grate as ordinary ground storage, seal the
loading entrance with mud, fuel the firebox and light it. A batch costs a fixed amount of fuel
energy whatever you burn — hotter fuel simply gets through it faster:

10 firewood — about 13 hours
10 peat bricks — about 8 hours
6 charcoal — about 6 hours

The firebox tells you what it is doing from across the yard: dark when empty, firewood in
the mouth when fuelled, glowing red coals while the chamber warms, and bright orange flame once
it is up to temperature. Look at it for hours of burn remaining and firing progress.

Opening it early
Break a seal while the kiln is hot and thermal shock shatters part of the load —
nothing below 450°C, up to about half the batch at full heat. That threshold is exactly
when the firebox turns from red to orange, so the colour is the warning. Whatever survives
keeps the heat it has taken, and a repaired kiln picks up where it left off.

The mud seals crack in every firing and must be replaced, which is the kiln's running cost:
throughput and fuel economy paid for in clay rather than firewood.
